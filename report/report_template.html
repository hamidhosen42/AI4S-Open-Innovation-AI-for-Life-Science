1. Problem definition and application scenario

1.1 Why a label-free nuclear channel matters on a chip

Organ-on-a-chip (OoC) systems rebuild tissue-level structure and function in microfluidic devices and are imaged repeatedly — often for days or weeks — by transmitted-light microscopy. Nearly every quantitative read-out derived from those images starts by locating nuclei: cell number, confluence, proliferation, apoptosis and pyknosis, nuclear morphology, compound dose–response. That step is usually done on a fluorescent DNA stain or a fluorescent histone reporter, each with a cost in the OoC setting:

  • Fixed stains end the experiment. DAPI after fixation gives one time-point per chip; a time course needs one chip per time-point.
  • Live DNA dyes perturb the cells they measure. Repeated excitation is phototoxic [1]; Hoechst 33342 is phototoxic in time-lapse imaging [11], and the live-cell dye SiR-Hoechst induces DNA-damage responses and impairs cell-cycle progression [12].
  • Reporter lines must be engineered — routine for a cell line such as the HeLa H2B line used here, but often impractical for primary or iPSC-derived tissue, e.g. the neural cultures that are the focus of the challenge's supporting organisation.
  • Each fluorescence channel spent on nuclei is unavailable for a functional reporter (e.g. a Ca²⁺ indicator), and every perfused reagent adds handling steps and bubble/contamination risk in small channels.

In-silico labeling (ISL) — predicting the fluorescence image from the label-free image with a neural network [2, 3] — can reduce routine staining once a paired training set exists for the system (§8), and is one of the three impact areas named by the organisers. What keeps ISL out of quantitative decisions is trust: a network returns an image for every input, including inputs unlike its training data, and nothing in a point prediction tells the biologist which pixels, nuclei or frames are wrong. ChipStain therefore asks a narrower and more useful question than "can we predict the stain?": can we predict it and say — per pixel, per nucleus and per frame — how much to trust it, and does that trust signal hold up against simple alternatives?

Target users are OoC and cell-culture laboratories and CROs running live or high-content assays, and platform developers who need a quality gate before automated analysis. Scope and honesty statement. All quantitative validation uses public data: 2-D HeLa monolayers in a 96-well imaging plate (main results, 60 h time-lapse) and human iPSC-derived motor-neuron cultures in a dish (transfer test). No organ-on-a-chip device data were available to us; OoC is the target deployment, and §6.7 tests how the model behaves under the imaging shifts a chip introduces.

Value case (illustrative arithmetic, stated assumptions)

A neural OoC culture imaged every 30 min for 14 days at 4 fields per chip needs 14 × 48 × 4 = 2,688 nuclear-channel exposures per chip; a label-free nuclear channel removes all of them and frees that channel for a functional reporter. Fixed DAPI would instead need one chip per time-point. Neurons are post-mitotic, so label-free nuclear counts and nuclear condensation over weeks serve as a viability/neurotoxicity end-point without repeated dye exposure. The cost of being wrong is mitigated by a frame-level σ gate (§6.10), which routes frames with unusually high mean σ to a person instead of a growth curve.

2. Related work and contribution

In-silico labeling. Christiansen et al. [2] predicted multiple fluorescent labels from transmitted-light z-stacks, outputting for every pixel a discrete probability distribution over 256 intensity values. Ounkomol et al. [3] (“fnet”) trained a deterministic 3-D U-Net with a mean-squared-error loss; adversarial virtual staining followed for histology [13]. Uncertainty in bioimage restoration. CARE [14] predicted per-pixel Laplace parameters and used the disagreement of an ensemble to flag unreliable regions; a recent diffusion-based ISL method derives hallucination maps from the variance across denoising steps [15]. Uncertainty estimation. Heteroscedastic regression models aleatoric noise [4]; its maximum-likelihood training is unstable and β-NLL [8] fixes this; deep ensembles [16] and test-time augmentation [17] add model-related uncertainty; sparsification curves and AUSE [18] score how well an uncertainty ranks errors.

Contribution. ChipStain introduces no new uncertainty mechanism. Its contributions are: (i) a β-NLL-stabilised heteroscedastic ISL model with test-time augmentation for bright-field → nuclei, released as an open, CPU-capable tool; and (ii) a validation of its uncertainty that the ISL literature rarely reports — against an equally strong TTA baseline, against uncertainty-free proxies (predicted intensity, edge strength, cell density), at pixel, nucleus and frame level, under imaging shift, on a biological read-out (doubling time) with a gate fixed on validation data, and on neural cultures. We report where σ helps and, equally, where it does not.

3. Data

3.1 Main dataset: source, licence, usage note

Automatic labelling of HeLa "Kyoto" cells using Deep Learning tools by Romain Guiet (EPFL BioImaging & Optics Platform), Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.6140064, CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) [5], derived from the time-lapse HeLa "Kyoto" cells under the scope (10.5281/zenodo.6139958, CC BY 4.0) [6]. HeLa "Kyoto" cells stably expressing EGFP-α-tubulin and mCherry-H2B were grown as a 2-D monolayer in a CellCarrier Ultra 96-well plate (PerkinElmer) and imaged on a PerkinElmer Operetta (20×/NA 0.8) every 15 min for 60 h. The archive provides, for 5 time-points (frames 1, 10, 50, 100, 150 ≈ 0–37 h) of every field, a 540 × 540 px 16-bit bright-field image, two digital-phase-contrast renderings, a two-channel fluorescence image and StarDist nuclei labels computed automatically on the H2B channel [10]. Input is bright-field; target is mCherry-H2B (channel 1: correlation with the binarised nuclei labels {{chan_h2b}} vs {{chan_tub}} for tubulin over the 200 training images; scripts/check_channels.py). Usage note. The provider asks that the fluorescence not be reused with the derived labels for training and notes that the automatic labels are imperfect. We comply: we train only bright-field → fluorescence, never on the labels, and use the StarDist labels solely as reference objects for evaluation. Segmentation F1 and count errors therefore measure agreement with StarDist run on the real stain, not with manual ground truth.

3.4 Pre-processing, compliance and ethics

Bright-field images are normalised per image with their 1st/99th percentiles (≈ [−2, 2]), making the model insensitive to camera offset and gain; the target is scaled with fixed constants from the training H2B distribution, (I − 600)/(20 000 − 600), clipped to [0, 1], and all image metrics are computed in this space. Training uses random 256 × 256 crops with 90° rotations and flips; evaluation uses full 540 × 540 images. Figures show percentile-normalised, colour-mapped renderings; every image is listed with its source and modifications in Appendix E. All data are public and CC BY 4.0. HeLa is an established human-derived cell line (taken from Henrietta Lacks in 1951 without her consent); the images contain no genetic, clinical or personal information. No clinical, personal or restricted data are used.

4. Method

4.1 Backbone

A U-Net [7] built with segmentation_models_pytorch [9]; ResNet-34 encoder [19] initialised from ImageNet [20]; five-stage decoder with skip connections; one input channel; ≈24.4 M parameters.

4.2 Heteroscedastic output and β-NLL

The last layer outputs two maps: the predicted mean μ(x) and log-variance s(x) = log σ²(x) of a per-pixel Gaussian over the target y. The Gaussian negative log-likelihood

LNLL = ½ Σp [ sp + (yp − μp)² · e−sp ]

is known to be unstable: pixels that are hard early in training receive a large σ, the gradient on their mean is down-weighted by e−s, and they are under-fitted [8]. β-NLL [8] multiplies each pixel's loss by the detached factor (σ²)β; we use β = 0.5 (β = 0 recovers the NLL; at β = 1 the gradient with respect to μ equals the MSE gradient while σ² is still fitted). Because the loss is Gaussian, the mean head is trained with a σ-weighted squared error rather than the L1 loss of our baseline — a confound we test explicitly (§6.3). s is clamped to [−10, 4]. σ is the model's learned (aleatoric) uncertainty.

4.3 Test-time augmentation, ensembles and the reported σ

At inference the network is applied to the eight dihedral transforms of the input (4 rotations × optional flip); outputs are mapped back and μ̄ is their mean. The reported uncertainty is σtot = √(mean of the eight σ² + variance of the eight μ). We call the second term TTA disagreement: it measures how much the prediction changes under symmetries the model should respect, a partial proxy for model uncertainty, not a Bayesian epistemic estimate [17]. For the plain U-Net baseline, the TTA disagreement alone is its uncertainty. We also evaluate 3-seed deep ensembles [16] built from the cached predictions of the three training seeds (σ² = variance of member means + mean member σ²).

4.4 From pixels to decisions

Per nucleus: nuclei are segmented on the prediction (Gaussian blur, Otsu threshold, distance-transform watershed); each nucleus receives the mean σ inside it. Per frame: the mean σ of the image, divided by the median over frames of the same time-point (cell density grows with time, and σ with density); a frame is flagged when this ratio exceeds its 95th percentile on the validation split — a 5 % false-alarm budget fixed without test labels.

4.5 Evaluation protocol

Fidelity: MAE, PSNR, SSIM, Pearson r against the real H2B. Downstream: object-level F1 at IoU ≥ 0.5 against the StarDist reference nuclei; the same pipeline run on the real H2B gives the reference level a perfect prediction would reach. Uncertainty ranking: Spearman ρ(σ, |error|) on a fixed 200 000-pixel sample per image; AUSE [18] (area between the sparsification curve and the oracle curve, normalised; lower is better); and the MAE drop after removing the 20 % highest-σ pixels — each also computed for two uncertainty-free proxies taken from the prediction itself: predicted intensity μ and its edge strength |∇μ|. Calibration: empirical coverage of μ ± zσ. Statistics: 3 training seeds per configuration. Inference rests on 95 % confidence intervals from a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples seeds and fields, and on per-seed field-level Wilcoxon tests; a difference is called robust only if its CI excludes zero, no seed goes the other way and at least two seeds are significant on their own. A pooled field-level Wilcoxon (Holm-corrected) is reported for the uncertainty metrics only.
