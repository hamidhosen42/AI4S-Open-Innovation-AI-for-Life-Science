ChipStain: uncertainty-aware label-free nuclear staining for organ-on-a-chip imaging

Abstract. Fluorescent nuclear stains are the workhorse read-out of organ-on-a-chip (OoC) experiments — cell counts, viability, proliferation and morphology all start from a nuclear channel — yet staining is phototoxic, terminates live time-lapse imaging and adds reagent and handling steps on small microfluidic devices. In-silico labeling (ISL) models predict the stain from label-free bright-field images, but existing ISL networks return a single image with no indication of where the prediction can be trusted, which is the main obstacle to using them for quantitative decisions. We present ChipStain, a bright-field-to-H2B translation model that predicts, for every pixel, both the fluorescence intensity and its uncertainty. The model is a U-Net with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 encoder and a heteroscedastic Gaussian head trained with the β-NLL loss; at inference, eight-fold dihedral test-time augmentation supplies an additional epistemic term. On a held-out well of the public HeLa "Kyoto" dataset (125 images, CC BY 4.0), ChipStain reaches Pearson r = {{o_pearson}} and SSIM = {{o_ssim}} against the real stain (plain U-Net: r = {{b_pearson}}, SSIM = {{b_ssim}}); nuclei segmented from the prediction match reference objects with F1 = {{o_segf1}}, versus {{real_segf1}} when the real fluorescence is segmented with the same pipeline. The uncertainty is informative: per-pixel σ correlates with the absolute error (Spearman ρ = {{o_rho}}), discarding the 20 % most-uncertain pixels lowers the remaining error by {{sparse_gain}} %, and per-image mean σ ranks images by accuracy with ρ = {{rho_img}}. Code, weights, a CPU inference script and a Gradio demo are released under MIT; all data are public and openly licensed.

1. Problem definition and application scenario

Organ-on-a-chip systems reconstruct tissue-level structure and function in microfluidic devices and are imaged repeatedly, often for days, by transmitted-light (bright-field or phase-contrast) microscopy. Nearly every quantitative read-out downstream of those images — cell number, confluence, proliferation rate, apoptosis, nuclear morphology, compound dose–response — starts by locating nuclei, and that step is normally done on a fluorescent DNA stain (Hoechst, DAPI) or a fluorescent histone fusion (H2B). Staining, however, has real costs in the OoC setting:

  • Phototoxicity and photobleaching. Repeated excitation of DNA dyes damages cells and perturbs the biology being measured [1].
  • End-point only. Fixation-based stains terminate the experiment; live dyes accumulate and limit the number of time-points.
  • Cost and handling on chip. Reagents must be perfused through small channels; every additional step risks bubbles, flow disruption and contamination, and increases per-chip cost.
  • Channel budget. Fluorescence channels are scarce; freeing one from the nuclear counterstain leaves it for a biologically informative marker.

In-silico labeling — predicting fluorescence from label-free images with a convolutional network [2, 3] — removes these costs, and is one of the three impact areas explicitly named by the challenge organisers. The obstacle to adoption is trust: a network produces an image for every input, including ones far from its training distribution, and a biologist has no way of telling a faithful prediction from a hallucinated one [4]. ChipStain therefore addresses a narrower and more useful question than "can we predict the stain?": can we predict the stain and say, per pixel and per image, how much to trust it?

Target users are OoC and cell-culture laboratories and CROs running live time-lapse or high-content assays who want label-free nuclear read-outs, and platform developers who need a quality gate before automated downstream analysis. Scope. We restrict the deliverable to one target channel (nuclei) on one public dataset with a strict well-level hold-out, so that every claim can be verified from the released code within minutes.

2. Data

2.1 Source and licence

We use the public dataset Automatic labelling of HeLa "Kyoto" cells using Deep Learning tools by Romain Guiet (EPFL BioImaging & Optics Platform), Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.6140064, released under CC BY 4.0 [5]. It is derived from the time-lapse dataset HeLa "Kyoto" cells under the scope (10.5281/zenodo.6139958) [6]. HeLa "Kyoto" cells stably express EGFP-α-tubulin and mCherry-H2B, and were imaged on a PerkinElmer Operetta (20×/NA 0.8, Andor Zyla 5.5) for 60 h at 15-min intervals. The archive contains, for each field of view and for five time-points (1, 10, 50, 100, 150), a 540 × 540 px 16-bit bright-field image, two digital-phase-contrast renderings, a two-channel fluorescence image (tubulin, H2B), and StarDist nuclei labels computed from the H2B channel. The data are a cell-line imaging dataset; they contain no human-subject, clinical or personal information.

2.2 What we use

Input is the bright-field channel; target is the mCherry-H2B channel (channel index 1, verified by its correlation of 0.79 with the nuclei labels versus 0.40 for tubulin). The StarDist labels are used only as reference objects for the downstream segmentation metric — never for training or model selection.

2.4 Pre-processing

Bright-field images are normalised per image with their 1st/99th percentiles (mapped to about [−2, 2]), making the model insensitive to camera offset and gain. The target is scaled with fixed constants from the training H2B distribution, (I − 600)/(20 000 − 600), and clipped to [0, 1]; all image metrics are computed in this space. Training uses random 256 × 256 crops with random 90° rotations and flips; evaluation uses the full 540 × 540 image, reflect-padded to 544.

3. Method

3.1 Backbone

ChipStain is a U-Net [7] built with segmentation_models_pytorch; the encoder is a ResNet-34 initialised from ImageNet weights, the decoder is the library's standard five-stage decoder with skip connections, and the input layer is adapted to one channel. The network has ≈24.4 M parameters and runs at ≈0.6 s per 540 × 540 image on CPU (≈5 s with test-time augmentation).

3.2 Heteroscedastic output and β-NLL loss

Instead of a single intensity, the last layer outputs two maps: the predicted mean μ(x) and the log-variance s(x) = log σ²(x) of a per-pixel Gaussian over the target y. The natural training objective is the negative log-likelihood

LNLL = ½ Σp [ sp + (yp − μp)² · e−sp ].

Plain NLL is known to be unstable: pixels that are hard early in training receive a large σ, their gradient on μ is down-weighted by e−s, and they never get learned. We use the β-NLL reformulation of Seitzer et al. [8], which multiplies the per-pixel loss by the detached factor (σ²)β with β = 0.5, interpolating between NLL (β = 0) and a plain MSE on μ (β = 1). The log-variance is clamped to [−10, 4] for numerical safety. The resulting σ(x) is the model's aleatoric uncertainty: the intrinsic ambiguity of inferring fluorescence from the bright-field appearance at that location (e.g. cells out of focus, overlapping nuclei, mitotic rounding).

3.3 Test-time augmentation and epistemic term

At inference we evaluate the network on the eight dihedral transforms of the input (4 rotations × optional flip), undo the transform on the outputs, and report μ̄ (mean of the eight μ) as the prediction. Two variance terms are combined: the mean of the eight predicted σ² (aleatoric) and the variance of the eight μ across views (a cheap proxy for epistemic uncertainty — disagreement of the model with itself). The uncertainty map is σtot = √(σ̄²ale + σ²epi).

3.4 Baselines and ablation

  • baseline_unet — same U-Net architecture with a randomly initialised encoder, one output, L1 loss (the standard ISL recipe of [2, 3]).
  • pretrained_l1 — ImageNet-initialised encoder, one output, L1 loss (isolates the effect of pre-training).
  • chipstain_nll — ImageNet encoder, two outputs, β-NLL (isolates the effect of the uncertainty head).
  • chipstain_nll + TTA — the full method.

3.5 Evaluation protocol

Image fidelity: MAE, PSNR, SSIM and Pearson r between prediction and real H2B, per image, averaged over the 125 test images. Downstream utility: we segment nuclei from the predicted image with a fixed, stain-agnostic pipeline (Gaussian blur σ = 1, Otsu threshold, small-object removal, distance-transform watershed) and match objects to the StarDist reference labels at IoU ≥ 0.5, reporting precision, recall and F1. Running the same pipeline on the real fluorescence gives the ceiling that a perfect prediction could reach. Uncertainty quality: (i) Spearman rank correlation between per-pixel σ and |error| on 200 000 random pixels per image; (ii) sparsification curves — remove pixels in decreasing order of σ and track the MAE of the remainder, compared with the oracle ordering by true error; we report the area between the two curves (AUSE, lower is better) and the error reduction after removing 20 % of pixels; (iii) image-level: Spearman correlation between mean σ and MAE across the 125 test images, which is the quantity a quality gate would use.

6. Reliability analysis and limitations

  • Single cell line, single microscope. All data come from HeLa "Kyoto" cells on one Operetta system. We hold out an entire well, which tests robustness to well-to-well variation but not to a different cell type, magnification or an actual OoC device. The per-image input normalisation and the uncertainty head are designed to make transfer safer, not to guarantee it; applying the released weights to a new OoC system requires fine-tuning on a small paired set (we recommend ≥ 50 fields) and re-checking the calibration numbers of §5.3.
  • Fluorescent reporter, not a dye. The target is mCherry-H2B, which labels chromatin like Hoechst/DAPI but with a different intensity distribution; the model predicts a normalised intensity, not absolute photon counts.
  • Uncertainty is not a guarantee. σ is well-correlated with error on in-distribution data ({{unc_summary}}), but a heteroscedastic head can be confidently wrong on inputs unlike anything in training; the TTA-based epistemic term mitigates but does not solve this. We therefore present σ as a triage signal, not as a calibrated confidence interval.
  • Segmentation ceiling. The downstream F1 is limited by our deliberately simple watershed pipeline (F1 = {{real_segf1}} even on real fluorescence); a stronger segmenter would raise both numbers. The comparison between predicted and real fluorescence under the same pipeline is the fair quantity.
  • Pre-training effect. On this dataset the ImageNet-initialised encoder did not outperform training from scratch under our schedule ({{pretrain_note}}); we report this honestly and keep it in the ablation.
  • Single seed. Numbers are from seed 0; per-image standard deviations are reported. Multi-seed runs are launched by scripts/run_all.sh 0 1 2.
  • Ethics. No human, clinical or personal data; the model cannot be misused for identification. Its main risk is over-trust in a hallucinated prediction — which is exactly what the uncertainty output is designed to counter.

7. Scientific and practical impact

For an OoC laboratory, ChipStain turns every bright-field frame of a time-lapse into a nuclear read-out, without the stain: cell counts and proliferation curves over days from the same chip, an extra free fluorescence channel, and lower reagent and handling cost. The uncertainty map is the component that makes this usable rather than merely impressive: a per-image mean σ threshold flags frames for human review; per-pixel σ can be propagated to per-nucleus confidence so that uncertain objects are excluded from a count or a dose–response curve; and σ maps highlight regions where the imaging itself (focus, density, debris) should be fixed. Because inference is CPU-only and the code depends on nothing proprietary, the model can sit inside an acquisition pipeline or a LIMS. In the organiser's road-map towards AI-driven OoC digital twins, a trustworthy label-free nuclear channel is a prerequisite: a twin fed with hallucinated observations is worse than no twin. The same formulation extends directly to further channels (tubulin is already in the dataset), to 3-D stacks, and to neural OoC cultures, which we list as next steps.
