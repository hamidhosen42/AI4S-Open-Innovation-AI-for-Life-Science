| 0:00–0:35 | **Slide 1** — title; a bright-field image next to its nuclear stain | "We are team Hack2Publish — Hamid, Esfer and Foysal — and this is ChipStain, in the Model & Algorithm category. Almost every read-out on an organ-on-a-chip — cell number, growth, viability — starts from a nuclear stain. But fixation ends the experiment, live DNA dyes are phototoxic, and reporter lines are rarely available for iPSC-derived tissue such as neurons. ChipStain predicts the nuclear stain from a plain bright-field image, and it also outputs a per-pixel uncertainty map, which we test as a quality signal." |
| 0:35–1:20 | **Slide 2** — architecture: bright-field → U-Net → mean μ and uncertainty σ | "The model is a U-Net whose head predicts, for every pixel, a mean and a variance, trained with the β-NLL loss so the variance does not swallow hard pixels. At test time we average eight flipped and rotated views; the spread between them — TTA disagreement — is added to the learned variance. Nothing here is a new mechanism. What we contribute is testing whether this uncertainty actually helps, against honest baselines." |
| 1:20–2:50 | **Live demo** — Gradio | "This is a bright-field image from a well the model never saw. With test-time augmentation the prediction appears in a couple of seconds on a laptop CPU, with the uncertainty map on the right: σ is higher on nuclei — brighter means noisier — and near zero on background. The app counts 183 nuclei; the dataset's reference annotation has 180. Now the same image, slightly blurred — a crude stand-in for defocus. The prediction falls apart, and the uncertainty jumps: mean σ goes from about 0.08 to 0.33, and the app warns that the input looks unlike its training data. A plain U-Net in this situation gets *less* uncertain — it fails silently." |
| 2:50–3:55 | **Slide 3** — results: table, sparsification curve, shift plot, growth curve | "Over three training runs on 125 test images, ChipStain matches a U-Net with the same augmentation in correlation with the real stain — 0.768 versus 0.764 — at a 10 percent higher pixel error. Its uncertainty ranks errors clearly better — Spearman 0.47 versus 0.28, in every test field and every seed — and it is close to calibrated: the 95 percent interval covers 92 percent of pixels. On a 60-hour time-lapse, counting nuclei on the prediction gives a doubling time of 24.9 hours against 23.9 from the real stain, and an uncertainty gate fixed on validation data cuts the doubling-time error from 9.1 to 4.1 percent. To be clear about the limits: if you only need a nuclear count, a segmentation model trained on matching labels does better — Cellpose reaches F1 0.831 versus our 0.708; per-nucleus σ is no better than a simple brightness heuristic; and we had no organ-on-chip images." |
| 3:55–4:30 | **Slide 1 again** + repo link | "On human stem-cell-derived motor neurons, the model transfers partly even without retraining, and fine-tuning on a single well raises the correlation from 0.59 to 0.76. Everything is open: code, weights — the seed-0 model — data links, a CPU inference script, and every number regenerated by one script. Thank you." |
